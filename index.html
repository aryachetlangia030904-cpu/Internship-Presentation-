What I learned

Five things this internship taught me.

01

Progress over perfection

Designs improve when they are put out, tested, and reviewed. Instead of waiting for the perfect solution, it is better to make progress, gather feedback, and keep refining.

02

Understanding beyond the brief

Interpreting a requirement literally isn't enough. I need to understand the intention behind it, balance what stakeholders want with what users need, and translate both into meaningful design decisions.

03

Designing with constraints

Good design doesn't always require major changes. On an existing product, small, thoughtful improvements can make a significant difference, especially when working with established systems.

04

Using AI as a design tool

AI helped me move from rough wireframes to high-fidelity designs and prototypes faster. I learned to use it to speed up execution while keeping the design thinking and decisions in my hands.

05

Understanding my interests

The internship strengthened my interest in educational products, and showed me how much I enjoy the creative side of design: storytelling, illustration, and visual exploration.

What I want to improve next

Where I want to grow from here.

01

Deeper problem understanding

Asking the right questions, understanding the intent behind feedback, and identifying the actual problem before jumping into solutions.

02

Technical understanding

Understanding AI tools and development workflows more deeply, including the differences between closed and open AI models, to make more informed design decisions.

03

Active listening and note-taking

Documenting meetings by identifying key decisions, priorities, and action items instead of relying entirely on transcripts, so I can separate what matters from background discussion.

04

Balancing creativity with usability

Knowing when to explore creative directions and when to simplify. A design should be distinctive, but it should also communicate clearly and respect the user's time.

How it works

  1. Create and print worksheets
  2. Students write on paper
  3. Teacher scans with a phone
  4. AI grades and gives analytics

Key features: handwriting digitisation, rubric-based grading suggestions, insights on student misconceptions, and a searchable record of progress, across maths, languages, science and social studies.

Keeping handwriting alive

Smart Paper believes “Paper is Smart.” Students keep writing by hand, and AI handles the work that comes after.

  • Why paper: about 20% better comprehension than screens, stronger memory, higher test scores, and no digital distractions.
  • Why it matters now: handwritten work is authentically the student's own in the age of AI.
  • Where AI helps: reading even messy handwriting, suggesting scores, spotting common errors and keeping digital records.

The result is a balance: technology supports the tradition of handwriting instead of replacing it.

How a feature gets built

From conversation to design

Teachers use Smart Paper to create classes and assessments, upload scanned answer sheets, and get results and analytics. Every feature starts with a conversation: the product manager, engineers and designer talk through how teachers work, what slows them down, and what to build or improve.

Ideas become rough sketches, then UI and UX flows, polished screens and clickable prototypes, with AI helping to try layouts quickly. Designs work across screen sizes, and the product manager signs off before anything is built.

From design to development

Engineers then work out the technical approach, from APIs and data to how the AI evaluates answer sheets, weighing accuracy, speed and scale. A good-looking solution is not useful if it is slow, unreliable or fails at scale.

Approved designs are built by reusing components from the codebase, writing new ones where needed, and connecting them to the backend. Each feature is tested, and the designer checks the built screens against the original designs.

Where I fit in

I am the designer in the process above, involved from the first conversation to the final design check.

  • Research and ideas: understanding how teachers work and bringing my own ideas to product discussions.
  • Product design: turning those ideas into sketches, UI/UX flows, responsive screens and clickable prototypes for PM sign-off.
  • Design review: checking built screens against the original designs before release.
  • Website design and storytelling: designing the website and shaping its story, giving the founder a clear, confident statement about his product.

where it started

User research & product planning

With the core product already in place, the focus was on understanding user needs, identifying opportunities, and planning future improvements.

  • User interviews: spoke with school and tuition teachers about their workflows, tools, and pain points, which showed the gap between personalised tuition needs and teaching at scale.
  • Competitive analysis: studied existing products to find feature gaps, understand market expectations, and explore expansion into the private education sector.
  • Feature prioritisation: consolidated existing and potential features into a planning framework, mapped to user needs, product goals, and feasibility.
The goal: move from individual user insights to a structured roadmap that balances user needs, business opportunities, and the realities of working at scale.

how I worked

My design process

1 · Understand the need

Talked to users and the product team to learn what they were really looking for. Most wanted minimal changes with more impact: the product runs at government level, so stability mattered more than novelty.

2 · Sketch it rough

Pen and paper to ideate flows. Figma when I needed to see how the UI would actually look.

3 · Let AI speed it up

AI turned rough wireframes into clean, high-fidelity screens and interactive prototypes. I stayed specific about what I wanted, without hand-prototyping every interaction.

The thinking stays mine. The execution gets faster.

user interface

Improving the onboarding experience

Problem: Many teachers logged in but failed to create their first assessment, making it difficult for them to get started with the product.

I proposed an onboarding experience to guide teachers towards their first assessment. After exploring different approaches, including a checklist inspired by tools like Claude and Notion, I shifted towards a more action-oriented approach.

  • Pre-onboarding: introduced a walkthrough to familiarise teachers with the key features before they entered the platform.
  • Guided homepage: introduced a homepage for new users, highlighting key actions like creating an assessment, creating a class, and scanning papers.
  • Making AI discoverable: brought the AI-assisted assessment creation option out of the hidden menu and made it more visible to help teachers get started.
  • Progress tracking: added a checklist that ticks off tasks as teachers complete them, helping them track their progress.
The goal was to reduce the effort required to get started and guide teachers towards creating their first assessment.
